**Maintained by:** Kirk
**Status:** Living document — update as the voice evolves

## The Voice

The game's narrative voice is its own thing. It doesn't sound like any other game. It sounds like Portland in 1992 told by someone who was there and mostly wasted for it.

### The Core Technique: Texture Then Brick

Build atmosphere with specific, sensory, almost literary detail. Layer it on — smells, sounds, the particular shade of water damage on a ceiling tile. Get the reader comfortable in the texture. Then hit them with something plain, blunt, and unavoidable. No flourish. No metaphor. A short sentence that lands like a right hook because they were busy looking at the wallpaper.

**Example — location description:**

> The Blue Parrot smells like decades of spilled beer fermented into the wood grain. The neon parrot in the window has one dead eye and the other flickers in a way that feels intentional, like it's winking at people it recognizes. There are four stools at the bar. One of them is broken. Nobody sits in the other three either.

**Example — event text:**

> You find a twenty-dollar bill on the sidewalk outside Plaid Pantry. It's wet. You don't want to think about why it's wet. You take it anyway. Twenty dollars is twenty dollars.

**Example — NPC introduction:**

> Carl has been sitting at the end of the bar at Mock's Crest since before you were born. He has a face like a catcher's mitt and opinions about everything. Most of them are wrong. He will tell you all of them.

### Rules

1. **Be specific. Always.** Not "a dirty bar." Not "a sketchy neighborhood." Name the stain. Describe the crack in the window. Specificity is what makes fiction feel like memory.

2. **Plain language hits harder than clever language.** Save the ten-dollar words for the texture. When it's time to land a point, use five-year-old vocabulary. "It's broken." "She's gone." "You're broke." "That hurt."

3. **Nobody in Portland thinks Portland is weird.** The narration treats everything as normal because it IS normal. A man walking a ferret on a leash past a strip club at 7am is not a joke. It's Tuesday.

4. **Funny without trying to be funny.** Never wink at the camera. Never set up a punchline. The humor comes from deadpan observation of genuinely absurd situations treated with complete sincerity.

5. **No euphemism.** If someone is drunk, they're drunk. If somewhere smells like piss, it smells like piss. The game uses plain words for plain things. This isn't HBO — we're not performing edginess. We're just not lying about what things are.

6. **Short sentences are weapons.** A paragraph of atmosphere followed by a four-word sentence is the game's signature move. Use it. Don't overuse it. A right hook only works because you don't see it coming.

7. **Every NPC is the main character of their own story.** Nobody exists to be quirky. Everyone has a reason they're at that bar at 2pm on a Wednesday. The game may never tell you what it is, but the writer has to know.

8. **Portland is beautiful and terrible at the same time.** The sunset over the Willamette is gorgeous. There's a guy face-down on the Burnside Bridge who might be dead. Both of these things are true simultaneously and neither one cancels out the other.

## Tone

Early 90s Portland. Not the Portland of Portlandia. The Portland where Tonya Harding lives. Where skinheads and anti-fascists fight in the streets. Where the crack epidemic left marks. Where the rents are so cheap that anyone can wash up here, and they do. The strip clubs outnumber the churches and both are doing brisk business.

This is a city that hasn't been discovered yet. It's not cool. It's just cheap and weird and violent and free in a way that doesn't exist anymore.

### No Ceiling

Nothing is off limits. Drugs, violence, sex work, addiction, homelessness, mental illness, crime — all present, all treated as facts of life, not spectacle. The game doesn't moralize. It doesn't celebrate suffering either. It just shows you Portland and lets you walk through it.

Craft over shock. Every dark moment earns its place by being true, not by being provocative. If it feels like it's trying to offend, it's written wrong. If it feels like it just... told you something you weren't ready to hear, it's written right.

## Psychology & Perception

### Insanities, Obsessions, Trauma

Every character — PC and NPC — can carry psychological weight. This is not a system of debuffs to be cured. It is personality. It is texture. It is what makes a person a person.

- **Trauma** is acquired through events. It never fully goes away. It changes what you notice, what options you have, how you react. A character who got jumped in the park sees the park differently forever.
- **Obsessions** are fixations that grow from repeated behavior. Drink enough and alcohol isn't just a choice, it's a gravitational pull. Spend enough time alone and solitude becomes a need. The game reflects this by weighting action menus — obsessions float to the top. They're always there. Whispering.
- **Insanities** are not clinical diagnoses. They are the game's word for when your perception of reality diverges from consensus. Maybe you're right and everyone else is wrong. The game doesn't judge. It just shows you what you see.

### Ambiguous Abilities

Some characters develop abilities that may or may not be real. The game never confirms. The game never denies. The results speak for themselves.

- Tied to altered states: drunk, stoned, exhausted, starving, manic, traumatized.
- Presented as real within the narrative. If you talk to a pigeon while stoned and the pigeon tells you where to find twenty bucks, and you go there and find twenty bucks — did the pigeon talk to you? The game describes the conversation. The game gives you the money. The game does not add a footnote.
- Some abilities are useful. Some get you arrested. Some do both.
- NPCs have these too. The guy at the church who says he can see your sins? Maybe he can. He knows things he shouldn't.

**Examples of ambiguous abilities:**
- Talking to animals (altered perception or actual communication)
- Reading emotions as colors (synesthesia or psychic ability)
- "Knowing" when something bad is about to happen (anxiety or precognition)
- Manifesting small changes in reality (coincidence or actual power)
- Hearing what people are thinking (paranoia or telepathy)
- Unnervingly accurate gut feelings about strangers

The system tracks these as abilities with real mechanical effects. The narrative wraps them in ambiguity. The player decides what they believe.

## Content Standards

### Location Descriptions

Every location needs:
- **Default description** — first or typical visit, neutral state
- **Time-of-day variants** — at minimum: day vs. night
- **State variants** — how it reads when you're drunk, exhausted, starving, etc.
- **Repeat visit flavor** — the description should evolve. Third visit to Mock's Crest shouldn't read like the first.
- **One specific sensory detail** that nobody else would think to include

### NPC Descriptions

Every NPC needs:
- **Physical description** — specific, not generic. Not "a tall man." WHICH tall man.
- **One habit or tic** — something they always do. Cracks knuckles. Talks to their drink. Blinks too much.
- **Voice** — how they talk. Short sentences? Big words used wrong? Never finishes a thought?
- **What they want** — even if the player never finds out
- **What they're afraid of** — even if the player never finds out

### Event Text

- Keep it tight. Events are interruptions — don't write a novel.
- Lead with what's happening, not how the player feels about it.
- End with a choice or a consequence, never with a shrug.
